Jami Mosque in the Roncesvalles neighbourhood of Toronto is a Presbyterian church acquired and repurposed by the local Muslim community in the 60s. It's the oldest Islamic centre in Toronto and a special building considering most other downtown mosques are converted commercial spaces.

The outside of the building still appears like a church, with barely any modification. Inside, the familiar smells of a church are no longer present. Carpets are laid out facing the side walls at a slight angle, aligning the worshippers with Mecca. On the second story, near the front entrance, is where the sisters pray, and the second level at the back contains a library and office of the Imam.

I photographed two Eid prayers during my travels in Central Asia, one in Kashgar and another in Almaty. After coming back to Toronto, I didn't want to miss the Eid prayers here.

I visited the mosque on the last night of Ramadan. There was a relatively small turnout with only two rows of worshippers. Before the prayer, snacks were provided to break the fast and a dinner was served after.

The next morning I came back for the Eid prayers. The light came in from the east through the large side windows, casting shadows on the people waiting for the prayers to start. More worshippers flowed into the building until it was full. People told me the mosques in the suburbs often require hosting up to three sessions on Eid to accommodate everyone, and that the turnout here is small in comparison.

Like all prayers I had seen in other countries, the sequence was no different. The difference was the multitude of cultures represented in the mosque.
